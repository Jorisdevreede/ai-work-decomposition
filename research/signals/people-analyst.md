# Signals: People analyst

Window: 16 July 2026 to 14 October 2026. Written 14 October 2026 by SCOUT.
Each link below was opened in this session. Grades: E2 a named product does this today; E3 a documented case or a legal source with a page you can read. Nothing here is E4. Vendor and marketing pieces are marked.

What shows up for this role: the live work is describing the tasks people actually do, and turning headcount, movement and skills into a decision a manager can use. The tools on sale answer a workforce question in chat, or draft a task list from email and calendar. The one measured internal deployment is Workday describing its own HR team, with no sample size. A separate survey from a people-analytics vendor says almost half of US employees exaggerate their AI use, which makes a self-reported adoption number a weak input. No opened page shows a Dutch employer with a forecast error, hours saved, or a works-council decision on a task dashboard.

## S1. Workday's own team built an agent that infers tasks from approved digital exhaust

- Date: the page does not print one.
- Source: https://blog.workday.com/en-us/how-workdays-hr-team-is-reimagining-workflows-with-sana.html
- Who: Phil Willburn, byline on the Workday blog. Carl Adams, Principal, Business Intelligence, is quoted. The builder is Workday's work intelligence team, using Sana. No sample size is printed.
- What: The agent reads employee-approved connections (email, meetings, documents, Salesforce, GitHub, Jira, calendar) and infers the tasks the person actually does. The employee checks that list in a short conversation, gets recommendations, and receives a Work Insights Profile: tasks, time, AI opportunities, goals, and prompts. Validated tasks go back to the work intelligence team for role modernisation, workforce insights and capacity planning. Willburn calls it an agentic time-in-motion study and says it cuts traditional HR research from months to minutes. Adams says Sana lets employees filter their digital exhaust into a task profile and check it. The same data is already in use on one organisation change: as more coding moves to agents, software testers are being placed in the same organisation as the engineers, and leaders compare what the agent tracks with day-to-day coding. Willburn says testers' output is catching up to engineers', and that AI speeds technical work more than coordination. Engineers who use AI most see the largest gains in output and morale, while their meeting time barely changes. He also writes that the hardest part, across the agents on this page, was measuring value after the agent existed, and that baselines should be set before launch. Responsible AI, legal and privacy review every agent through an HR AI Hub. The page does not say the agent rates a person, sets a band, or replaces a manager's decision.
- Number: 84% of employees rated the task capture extremely accurate. 93% gave the conversational review the same rating. 64% believe it will significantly change their daily work. No denominator, no method, no baseline for "months to minutes", and no figure for the tester move or for meeting time.
- Grade: E3 as the vendor's account of its own HR team. The three percentages are the vendor's, and the page does not show how they were collected.
- Flag: Workday is writing about Workday. "Months to minutes" is a slogan. Employee approval of a mailbox connection does not answer a Dutch works-council question. See S7.

## S3. Visier: nearly half of US employees in its survey exaggerate their AI use

- Date: CIO Dive, 23 September 2026. The Visier blog by Dr Andrea Derler does not print a date. CIO Dive says the report came out that Wednesday and links the blog.
- Source: https://www.ciodive.com/news/employees-performative-AI-pressure/831203/
- Who: Paige Gross, CIO Dive. Andrea Derler, principal of research and value at Visier. The blog is https://www.visier.com/blog/ai-in-the-workplace-new-research-on-performative-ai/. On X, @HRCurator reposted that blog URL several times in the window, including https://x.com/HRCurator/status/2107704976713671105 on 7 October 2026. Those posts share the link. They do not add a measurement.
- What: Visier used a third-party outlet, which it does not name, to survey 1,000 US full-time employees on daily AI use and on what they think of their employer's plan, then interviewed people in healthcare, finance, HR, software and other work. Derler told CIO Dive that the experience depends on the person's workflow. She also said that if people cannot be honest about what fails, they answer that they use AI for everything and that everything is better, and that this wastes time. The finding for this role is narrow: a count of people who say they use AI is an inflated count.
- Number: from the Visier blog. 48% have exaggerated their AI use or expertise to colleagues or leaders at least some of the time. 45% feel pressure to use AI when they are not confident. 54% say their role or career has significantly changed because of AI in two years, and 44% say it has not. 51% say the day-to-day workflow has changed significantly or moderately. 59% do not believe, or are unsure, that the employer has a clear plan (32% say there is no clear plan, 27% are unsure). Seven in 10 are concerned about a negative career effect. Job loss leads that list at 24%. Only 28% say leaders fully understand how employees use AI. 33% think leaders understand the training need. 29% think leaders recognise the concerns. 21% strongly agree the employer provides the training, resources and support to adapt. Asked what would raise confidence: 32% more training, 31% more transparency, 24% more advancement, 18% clearer communication, 12% say nothing would. The blog also cites an earlier planning study (87%, 29%, 56%, 15%). That page was not opened. Those four figures are not this survey. CIO Dive, on the same story, quotes a TrustedTech survey (41% taught themselves, 15% had employer training). That is a different survey and is not merged here. A clarification line on the CIO Dive page spells the firm "Vizier" once. The body says Visier.
- Grade: E3 as journalism of a vendor survey, and as the vendor's own write-up. The sample is 1,000 US full-time employees. The page does not give fieldwork dates, a response rate, or a sampling method.
- Flag: Visier sells people analytics, including the agent in S4. Do not read 48% as the share of Dutch staff, or as proof that a particular dashboard is wrong. It is evidence that self-report is a bad adoption metric.

## S5. Workday's October workforce report: leaders expect job content to move more than headcount, and the release says the link to AI is descriptive

- Date: 5 October 2026, PR Newswire. A Learning News page on the same report was also opened. Its retrieved text sits in the 2026 news section and does not print a day. A search index dates that page 9 October 2026. The report PDF behind Workday's form was not opened.
- Source: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/workday-global-workforce-report-ai-is-rewriting-jobs-more-than-its-cutting-them-302898076.html
- Who: Phil Willburn, vice president, people systems, intelligence and support, Workday. Learning News: https://learningnews.com/news/learning-news/2026/more-leaders-expect-ai-productivity-gains-than-workforce-cuts
- What: The release combines three kinds of material. De-identified workforce data from active Workday customers with at least 250 employees, matched year over year, for internal moves, promotions, turnover and retention. A September survey of employees and business leaders for what leaders expect from AI and for skill confidence. A separate AI@Work Pulse for how workers use AI in the job and in a job search. Skills demand comes from requisitions at employers using Workday Recruiting, September 2025 through July 2026. Willburn's line is that employees may not be changing jobs, but the jobs are changing around them. Learning News adds limits that the wire leaves easy to miss. Respondents could tick more than one expected effect, so the leader percentages are not slices of one pie. Workday's customer data does not establish how much of any headcount decline was caused by AI. The largest employers account for most of the net decline, and the typical employer's growth is about zero. A drop in advertised demand for basic AI skills does not establish that those skills became less useful. They may have become assumed. Workday calls the trends descriptive.
- Number: 40% of business leaders expect AI to get more from the people they already have. 28% expect it to reduce headcount. Learning News, citing page 12 of the report and the decision-maker questions, also lists 34% expecting changes to roles or work, 33% expecting new roles or skills, 18% expecting less need to recruit, and 8% expecting no significant impact. The release says the September survey has 6,001 employees and business leaders, of whom about 1,780 are decision-makers, and in another sentence says 6,000 full-time employees. Learning News places that survey in the US, UK, Canada, Australia, France and Germany. Inside it, 79% say they know which skills they need, 66% say the employer helps them develop those skills, and 65% are confident they can learn a new skill if asked. Internal moves fell at 57% of employers year over year. Promotion rates stayed essentially flat. Nearly 4 in 10 employees said their company reorganised in the past year. Voluntary turnover is roughly 16% a year. About 70% of employees are still with the same company two years later. In every industry analysed, people who plan to stay are less likely to recommend the employer. The gap reaches 11 points in the public sector. On requisitions across over 550 employers (Learning News says 554), demand for basic AI skills such as simple prompting peaked in January 2026 and then fell 25%. Demand for building AI tools, automating workflows and AI engineering rose 51% from September 2025 to July 2026. Mentions of management and leadership skills fell 7%. Mentions of training skills fell 13%. The Pulse survey has 5,944 workers. Among those who use AI for nearly all their work, 62% think it will make their current skills less valuable, and 76% expect it to open new career opportunities. 84% of job seekers said they used AI in the search. The median number of applicants per filled role rose from 58 to 69. Time to fill held at about 60 days. Applicants per filled job rose 27% in financial services and 40% in technology and media. In those two sectors, 53% and 57% of applicants said AI increased how many roles they applied to.
- Grade: E3 as a vendor release about its own customer base and its own surveys. Learning News is a second reading of the same report, and it is the page that states the multiple-response rule and the causality limit.
- Flag: Workday sells the system the workforce figures come from. The customers are Workday customers with at least 250 employees, not a Dutch labour-market sample. Posted skill demand is not a skill the new hire was judged to have. The Dutch retellings of this release that a search returned did not render a body, so they are not used.

## S6. A Dutch skills-matching vendor says a headcount forecast still fails to name who could do the work

- Date: 5 October 2026.
- Source: https://www.hrpraktijk.nl/hr-tech/datagedreven-hr/ai-en-de-toekomst-van-werk-er-is-geen-tekort-aan-talent-maar-een-tekort-aan-zicht-op-inzetbaarheid-en-ontwikkelbaarheid/
- Who: Laurens Waling, chief evangelist at 8vance, interviewed by HR Praktijk. 8vance has worked on AI matching since 2013, according to the page.
- What: Waling's claim is that people analytics has mostly explained the past at group level, and that the useful next question is individual and forward-looking: what work a person can do now, what else fits, what they could learn quickly, and where they might be deployable in six or twelve months. He treats a skills list as an input, not the answer. His illustration of today's planning is a sentence of the form "in three years we need 300 people of profile X and we will be about 70 short", which still does not name the people. He says a dashboard can say a shortage is coming, and that a useful agent would also propose who could fill it and what they still lack, with the employee, recruiter or manager keeping the decision. The named example is De Nieuwe Arbeidsmarkt, run by De Riverboard in the Waterweg region (Schiedam, Vlaardingen, Maassluis), with 8vance as technology partner. Employers look at the local market together. With the person's consent, someone who came second at one employer can be shown to another. The page gives no count of matches, no fill rate, and no error rate. "There is no shortage of talent, there is a shortage of visibility" is his line.
- Number: the 300 and the 70 are his example of how a workforce plan is usually phrased. They are not a count from a named employer. No other figure is on the page.
- Grade: E3 as a published interview with a named practitioner. E1 for the claim that a model can already predict six-to-twelve-month deployability. The regional project is described, not measured.
- Flag: Waling sells 8vance. An agent that proposes a person for a task is the sort of system S8 puts on the high-risk list once that list's duties apply, and the consent step in S7 is separate from the person's consent to be shown to a second employer.

## S7. Measuring who uses which AI tool is, on the Dutch regulator's test, a staff-tracking system

- Date: 28 September 2026 for the argument. The regulator page it relies on was last edited on 9 April 2025, which is before this window. Both were opened.
- Source: https://cruxdigits.nl/blog/shadow-ai-detectie-instemmingsrecht/
- Who: Tom Joseph, AI consultant at Crux Digits (the firm name on the page; the domain is cruxdigits.nl). He writes for IT and security leads at Dutch employers of about 250 to 5,000 staff. The Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens page: https://www.autoriteitpersoonsgegevens.nl/en/themes/employment-and-benefits/works-council/the-works-council-and-staff-tracking-systems
- What: Article 27(1)(l) of the Works Councils Act gives the works council a consent right over a scheme for facilities aimed at, or suitable for, observing presence, behaviour or performance. The AP page says a system the employer does not currently use to monitor staff is still a staff-tracking system if it could be used that way. Its examples include software that records internet use, and logging of who opened which file. If the council withholds consent, the employer asks the sub-district court (Article 27(4) WOR). Joseph applies that test to shadow-AI controls. A per-user report of which AI apps someone used, a browser control that classifies prompt text per user, and an insider-risk score per employee are, on his reading, inside Article 27(1)(l). Installing the tool in audit mode does not wait for the day someone opens the dashboard, because the system is already suitable for monitoring. An aggregate total does not settle the question if the tool still holds a row per person. Running the console in an IT partner's tenant does not move the decision off the employer. He separates two things that do not need that consent: offering one sanctioned tool with a processing agreement, and a written rule about which data must never be pasted, provided the rule is about the data and not about checking staff. A domain block with no per-user report is, on his reading, an advice right under Article 25(1)(k). Employee consent is a weak basis under the GDPR in an employment relationship, and it does not replace the works-council step. He says skipping the step can make the decision void. The page says this is not legal advice.
- Number: none on the Dutch rule. Joseph mentions a PagerDuty survey from June 2026 and says it has no Dutch respondents. That survey is outside this window and was not opened. Its percentages are not used.
- Grade: E3 for the AP's statement of Article 27(1)(l). E3 for Joseph's application of it to AI-use measurement, with the flag below. The AP page does not mention AI.
- Flag: Crux Digits sells AI implementation and says so at the foot of the page. Joseph is not a lawyer. For this role the operational point is narrow. A dashboard of who used a model, or a task profile built from email, calendar and chat, is the facility the consent right is about. The Workday design in S1, where each employee approves a connection, does not by itself meet that test.

## S8. Performance monitoring and task allocation from individual behaviour sit in Annex III, with the heavy duties delayed to 2 December 2027

- Date: 5 October 2026. The page is marked as at that date.
- Source: https://www.mtrlegal.com/ki-im-arbeitsverhaeltnis-neue-eu-fristen-fuer-arbeitgeber/
- Who: MTR Legal, a German law firm. No author is named.
- What: Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI, has been in force since 27 July 2026. For this role the operative part is Annex III point 4, read with Article 6(2). MTR lists systems intended for recruitment and selection, including targeted job ads, filtering applications and scoring candidates. It also lists systems intended to influence decisions on terms, promotion or termination, systems that allocate tasks from individual behaviour or personal traits, and systems that observe and evaluate the performance and behaviour of people in a job. The duties in Chapter III, sections 1 to 3, for a standalone Annex III system apply from 2 December 2027. The earlier date was 2 August 2026. Product-safety high-risk systems under Annex I move to 2 August 2028. Emotion recognition from voice, facial expression or face at work is already banned under Article 5, and that ban does not wait for December 2027. A people-analytics chatbot that a worker talks to is already an Article 50 transparency question. The page is the firm's reading. It is not a judgment, and it is not the Dutch works-council rule in S7.
- Number: the dates above. No fine is calculated on this page for a people-analytics deployment.
- Grade: E3 as a law firm's reading of the regulation.
- Flag: MTR sells employment advice. A headcount question in a chat is not, on this page, the same thing as a system whose purpose is to watch performance or to hand out tasks from a behavioural profile. The Workday agent in S1 infers tasks and feeds capacity planning. The page there does not say it scores people. If that inference is later used to allocate work or to judge output, S8 is the list it has to be read against, from 2 December 2027. Inferring emotion from a meeting is a current ban, not a 2027 question.

## What this role should not treat as settled

No opened page shows a Dutch employer running an AI workforce model with a forecast error, hours saved, or a works-council decision on the dashboard. Workday's 84%, 93% and 64% have no sample size. Microsoft's two agents have no result. Vee's "10x" and "over 2 million users" are lines on a product page. Visier's 48% is a US employee survey run for the vendor that sells the agent, and it is a warning about self-report, not a Dutch baseline. Workday's 40% and 28% can be ticked together, come from Workday customers and a six-country survey, and do not show that AI cut headcount. Waling's 300 and 70 are an illustration. The Crunchr customer page a search returned for a Dutch construction firm responded "page not found", so that case is not used. A Van Oers page on AFAS Profit 8 HRM did not return a body.

A task list inferred from email is not a pay band and not a calibration meeting. An employee clicking "approve this mailbox" is not the works council's consent. The high-risk duties for performance monitoring are not in force yet. The ban on inferring emotion at work already is.
